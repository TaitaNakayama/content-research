# Title-hook analysis — Xander 73 (奇軒Tricking), 48 Shorts

**Why this file exists.** For `taita.del` the transcript method largely fails, and that
is a finding rather than a gap. The best-performing tricking content is close to silent:
Storror's 97.6M-view rooftop Short carries only 106 recognised words, and Xander 73's
million-view Shorts are wordless. The hook in this format does not live in speech. It
lives in the **title**, which is the one thing about these videos that is fully
observable from this machine.

**Source and method.** His channel's Shorts tab was read directly
(`youtube.com/channel/UCvw1LiGdyulhnGksJlGWB6g/shorts`) on **2026-11-05** and the 48
Shorts it lists were parsed from the page's own data. View counts are YouTube's own
rounded labels ("92M views"), so they are approximate by YouTube's choice, not by mine.
Nothing here is estimated or inferred from a listicle.

## The finding that should change what you do

| **Chinese** | 16 | **204,000** | 1,200,000 | 4,000 |

English-titled Shorts run at **3.4x the median**
of his Chinese-titled ones, and every single video above 2M views is English-titled. The
one Chinese video that clears 1M is a paid Netflix promotion for 《乩身》, i.e. not organic
reach. He is a Taiwanese creator in Taiwan, posting to a mostly non-Chinese-reading
audience, and the title language is doing the work.

**What this means for you.** You are bilingual, and the instinct is to post Japanese for a
Japanese audience. This is direct evidence against that for the tricking account. Write
the title in English; put Japanese in captions or on-screen if you want it. It matches the
separate finding that there is no large Japanese tricking creator and the global tricking
field is small — Japanese is your authority and community play, not your reach play.

## Title patterns, ranked by the views they actually got

| Pattern | Shorts | Median views | Best example |
|---|---|---|---|

| ultra-short + emoji | 20 | 259,500 | "Level up 🔥" (92M views) |
| other | 9 | 187,000 | "Tell me your country." (767K views) |

Four things repeat across the winners, and all four are free to copy:

1. **He asks the viewer a question, in the title.** "Who else likes my outfit?" (38M, and
   reused for 13M), "Do you want me to go to India?" (10M), "What is your name?" (2M),
   "Who wants to be my student?" (2.8M), "Tell me your country." (767K). The video is
   silent; the title is what makes the comment section move.
2. **He sets up a reversal.** "He thought he won" (19M), "They laughed at her... until
   this" (709K), "Plot twist: She is the master" (1M), "Never laugh too early." (4.8M),
   "New Coach vs. Old Coach" (289K). The title promises a turn, the clip delivers it.
3. **He reuses titles that work.** "Who else likes my outfit?" twice (38M, 13M) and
   "Level up 🔥" twice (92M, 266K). He is not chasing novelty; he is re-firing a proven
   line. Note also how wide the spread is on the same title — the title is necessary, not
   sufficient.
4. **The best performer is three words.** "Level up 🔥" did 92M. Short beats clever.

## Your crash-and-landing arc maps straight onto pattern 2

The Kobe double-backflip crash and the landing years later are already a reversal; they
have simply never been packaged as one. The titles above are the template:

- crash clip → *"Everyone thought I was done"*
- landing clip → *"They laughed at this crash. Four years later."*
- the pair, pinned together → *"Plot twist: I landed it"*

That is the same machine as "He thought he won" at 19M, applied to footage you already own.
